Section 1 · The problem

A system that reaches almost every mother once, and hears her rarely

Kazakhstan does not have an access problem in the usual sense. Almost every pregnant woman sees a skilled provider and almost every baby is born in a facility. Its problem is what happens between those contacts.

The headline numbers are good. In the 2024 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS), 91.9% of women had at least four antenatal visits and 99.5% gave birth in a health facility.1 The Ministry of Health reports that maternal mortality fell by 12% and infant mortality by 11% in 2024.3

Three facts complicate them. First, many deaths are still avoidable: a confidential audit cited by UNFPA judged about 70% of maternal deaths preventable, and 10–12% happened at home, outside any facility.6 Second, progress is uneven. In August 2025 the Health Minister named Akmola's maternal mortality as about seven times the national value and listed six regions where it was rising.4 In the first quarter of 2026, infant mortality in rural areas rose from 6.26 to 6.43 per 1,000 while it fell in cities from 5.70 to 5.02.5 Third, the population at stake is large and shrinking: births fell from 388,400 in 2023 to 335,000 in 2025.7

Contact is also rare by design. Since September 2025 the universal home-visiting model gives two nurse visits during pregnancy and ten across early childhood, with more only when a risk is spotted.56 From my own experience as a mother, babies are seen at the polyclinic about once a month until their first birthday and much less often after, so a newborn goes weeks unseen at the age when danger signs move fastest. In villages the gap is wider: at the end of 2024 Kazakhstan was short of about 3,986 doctors and 4,231 mid-level staff,59 and in some rural districts doctor staffing barely reaches 75%.57 Paying staff to move has not fixed it: a 2024 study found the rural incentive scheme contributed insignificantly to rural workforce growth, while the density of rural pediatricians fell.60

Who is affected: pregnant women and mothers of infants under one year, with the highest stakes for rural women, women in regions with weak antenatal follow-up, women far from the regional perinatal centers where half of all births now happen,6 and women whose health-insurance (OSMS) status has lapsed: registration, basic doctor care and emergency care are free regardless, but planned prenatal screening and fetal ultrasound require insured status.15 The state pays contributions for non-working pregnant women once they register,72 but only for women without income, and status can change month to month.73 Where: nationwide, with clear regional hotspots. How it is changing: deaths are falling overall, but regional and urban–rural gaps are not closing evenly, and the COVID years showed how fast the numbers can reverse.

The social-science question

Why does a state that touches nearly every mother still fail to hear her in the hours that matter, and if an AI can listen, to whom should it report what it hears?

This is a question about institutions and power, not only about medicine. Kazakhstan's health system sees mothers through scheduled contacts and registries. President Tokayev's "listening state" promises to hear citizens, but in practice it hears those who call, complain or turn up. A frightened mother typing at 3 a.m. is invisible to both. Any AI that lets her be heard also makes her visible to the state, and that trade-off runs through the rest of this project.

Section 2 · Background and data

Lots of counting, little listening

Kazakhstan measures maternal and newborn health from three directions. Each sees part of the picture, and none sees the hours between visits.

Registration data. The Bureau of National Statistics (BNS) publishes births, infant deaths and maternal deaths by region in its quarterly "Natural movement of the population" spreadsheets and its Taldau database. The Ministry of Health's research center (NSCHD) publishes a yearly statistical book covering 2023–2024 with regional health indicators.16

Survey data. UNICEF's MICS 2024 interviewed 22,454 households and 14,365 women and is designed to give estimates for all 20 regions and for urban and rural areas.1

International models. WHO and UN agencies publish modeled maternal and child mortality, but only nationally.

Figure 1 changes how the problem should be scoped. "Rural women can't reach care" is not what the data show: rural women attend almost as often as urban women. The big differences are between regions, and they concern how often and how early women are seen. Only 26.1% of women in Aktobe had eight or more visits, against 98.8% in Abay. The same is true after birth. UNICEF reports that in Almaty region about nine in ten newborns get a medical check within a week of discharge,19 in Shymkent 80.4%,18 while Akmola ranks 18th, with nearly half of newborns missing that check.20 All three figures use the same measure, a medical check within the first week after discharge, taken from UNICEF's regional summaries of MICS 2024. They should not be mixed with the standard MICS "postnatal health check", which is close to universal because it mostly counts the check in the maternity hospital.1 A newborn's first week at home looks very different depending on the oblast.

How far can the numbers be trusted?

Four problems, from how births are defined to how few deaths are ever reviewed. The meter shows how much each one can distort the picture.

Definitions changed
Trends before 2008 are not comparable

In 2008 Kazakhstan adopted WHO live-birth criteria (from 22 weeks and 500 g). Counting the 500–999 g babies alone raised that year's infant mortality by about 21% and perinatal mortality by about 38%.8

Deaths can be reclassified
Newborn deaths may be undercounted

Between 2014 and 2018, stillbirths during labor among babies under 1,000 g rose from 7.9 to 99.3 per 1,000; UNFPA links punitive oversight to distorted statistics and found hospital transfers in only 0.2% of birth-registry records.6 One possible reading, contested and unproven, is that some early newborn deaths are recorded as stillbirths, which keeps them out of infant mortality.

Audits are thin
Few deaths are ever reviewed

The 2024 pilot of the perinatal death audit reviewed only 3.2% of the year's perinatal deaths, and three regions sent nothing, reportedly because hospitals feared blame.13

Numbers are small
Regional rates jump year to year

Maternal mortality jumped from 13.7 per 100,000 in 2019 to 36.5 in 2020 and 44.7 in 2021 because of COVID-19,10,11 then fell to about 10 by 2023–2024.21,62 In single regions, a handful of deaths swings the rate: North Kazakhstan went from 0 in 2019 to 76.7 in 2020.12 Even the best survey struggles, as Figure 2 shows.

Section 3 · Current state of research

Five ways of studying the problem, and what none of them asks

Research on this problem falls into five approaches. Each answers a different question, and the gap appears where they fail to meet. Tags show where the studies come from.

Public health
Kazakhstan

Counting deaths: Kazakh public-health epidemiology

Kazakhstani researchers have mostly studied the problem by auditing official numbers. Chuvakova and Karin measured how the 2008 live-birth definition inflated infant and perinatal mortality.8 Zhamantayev et al. showed that 76% of the 2020 maternal deaths were from indirect causes, mainly COVID-19.10 Nukeshtayeva et al. mapped regional maternal mortality from 2000 to 2020.12 Marat et al. evaluated the perinatal death audit and found inadequate care in 93% of the reviewed cases, but reviews covered only 3.2% of deaths.13

Program evaluation
KazakhstanCentral AsiaGlobal

Evaluating mobile health programs

A second body of work, mostly from sub-Saharan Africa, asks whether messaging mothers changes behavior. Kenya's PROMPTS was tested in a cluster randomized trial with 6,139 women in 40 facilities across eight counties. It raised knowledge, routine care-seeking and newborn care by small amounts, and 7.4 percentage points more women received the recommended postnatal visits; but it did not significantly change how women acted on danger signs, and the trial measured no deaths. It tested the whole service, so the AI's own contribution is not separated out.24,71 A widely quoted "2.28 times more likely" from a non-randomized Nairobi study is a misreading: 2.28 is an odds ratio for any antenatal visit (96% against 89%); for four or more visits it was 76% against 69%, not significant, among women who chose to enroll.25 South Africa's MomConnect, run by the national Department of Health, has a series of evaluations in BMJ Global Health.31 Kazakhstan has tried parts of this too: in 2017 Karaganda region launched a free prenatal app with an SOS button that sent a signal straight to the ambulance station,65 and Kazakhstani engineers have designed pregnancy apps for risk assessment under national medical rules.66 In Kyrgyzstan, WHO and UNFPA have backed apps for midwives and for pregnant women.76,77 I found no published evaluation of any of them.

Falls shortNo trial has shown fewer deaths, or better action on danger signs. Rigorous tests are mostly from Africa; Central Asian attempts were launched or designed but not evaluated, and success is measured by enrollment more than by emergencies caught.
Computer science
KazakhstanGlobal

Engineering triage: can a machine sort urgent messages?

Computer scientists ask a narrower question. For MomConnect, a simple classifier sorted help-desk messages with 85.4% accuracy.30 For PROMPTS, TRIM-AI's authors report about a 17% gain over the old system in mixed English and Swahili, from a two-week test of 5,323 messages rated by mothers for usefulness; the 12% cut in workload is the organization's own estimate. Neither measures how many real emergencies are caught.27,28 The closest work to Ana is Kazakhstani: MamaCare, published on 5 November 2026, is a language-model platform for perinatal mental health that screens and triages red flags in Kazakh, Russian and English. Two physicians rated its replies in 286 test sessions (109 in Kazakh, 90 in Russian, 3 mixed) and rated accuracy and safety highly; its main advantage was fewer false alarms, not more emergencies caught.67

KnownAI can prioritize a queue for humans.
ContestedThe error rate that remains; Jacaranda Health has said its automated replies are wrong about 30% of the time, without saying what kind of errors or how serious, which is why a human takes over.26
Falls shortThe Kazakh and Russian tests so far use sessions written by researchers, with no real patients;67 no one has measured how often such triage misses a real emergency here.
Medicine and human-computer interaction
Global

Testing chatbots as medical advisers

The newest strand asks whether general chatbots can advise patients. Ayers et al. found that health professionals preferred ChatGPT's answers to physicians' answers in 78.6% of evaluations, for quality and empathy.32 But Bean et al., in a randomized study published in Nature Medicine, found that when real people used the same models, they identified relevant conditions in fewer than 34.5% of cases and chose the right course of action in fewer than 44.2%, no better than a control group without AI.33 In pregnancy specifically, a 2025 conference study found misinformation in 27% of 84 ChatGPT answers.34 WHO's 2024 guidance responds with over 40 recommendations, including independent audits of health AI.35

Falls shortAlmost no tests with real mothers in Central Asia, in Kazakh or Russian.

The gap this project speaks to

Kazakhstan already has pieces of Ana: an SOS app in Karaganda, engineering designs, and now MamaCare's bilingual triage. None has been tested with real families. So the open question is narrower than "can AI work in Kazakh?": does bilingual AI triage linked to an on-duty nurse help real families get help faster, and how often does it miss a dangerous case? Local epidemiology counts deaths, not what happens between contacts; governance research has not yet heard from citizens. No study I found asks what happens when such a tool is tied to a state identity number in a centralized, "listening" state rather than run by an NGO, as PROMPTS is. Evaluations count enrollment more readily than missed emergencies, which is how Supermom Academy could report 400,000 users and still disappear. This project designs for that question: judged by red flags caught, time to help and data kept safe, not sign-ups.

Section 4 · The AI-assisted solution

Ana: a question-answering assistant that knows when to call a human

My design does one thing: it answers pregnant women's and new mothers' questions in Kazakh and Russian, tells them whether and how urgently to see a doctor, and when it spots a red flag, tells the mother to call 103 and alerts a human nurse who calls her back.

What kind of system. A chat assistant (working name Ana, "mother" in Kazakh) built on ISSAI's open-source KAZ-LLM, a Kazakh–Russian–English language model developed at Nazarbayev University,42 hosted on servers in Kazakhstan. It does not answer from the open internet: it answers from the Ministry of Health's clinical protocols and an answer bank written and reviewed by Kazakhstani obstetricians and pediatricians.

Doing what. Every message is sorted into three levels: green (information), amber (see your polyclinic within a day or two, with help booking) or red (call 103 now). When a child is ill, the mother logs each vomit, temperature or dose in one tap and Ana counts it all for the doctor.

Run by whom. The Ministry of Health, through a hybrid: a small remote 24/7 nurse desk, staffed from cities and possibly sitting with existing lines such as iKomek 109, plus the family's own patronage nurse or village feldsher in the morning.

Reaching whom. In the pilot, any pregnant woman or mother of a baby under one, who registers herself with her IIN and city. With her consent, and where lawful access to the attachment register allows, Ana can also tell her which polyclinic she is attached to and show it to the nurse. The IIN does not say where she is: 103 needs her address, a landmark and a phone number, and helps everyone regardless of attachment. Whether the IIN speeds up help is for the pilot to test. The first year is where the risk is highest and the evidence strongest. If the pilot meets its targets, Ana expands to mothers of children under three, matching the age range of the patronage home-visit model, when check-ups become rarer and the gaps between visits get longer.

Why it has to be trained locally

General chatbots are good at textbook medicine, but they learn mostly from English-language, Western sources. In my own use they sometimes default to other countries' norms: feeding advice built around other countries' diets and baby-food products, or illnesses that are rare in Kazakhstan, which WHO has listed as malaria-free since 2012.58 And no Kazakhstani doctor is accountable for what they say. Ana is grounded in local protocols and checked by local doctors.

One night in a village

Ana does not replace local services; it routes to them. A small remote nurse desk, staffed from cities where staffing is better, covers the night. The bill

Economic: wages are only the part I can count

Staffing depends on how many red-flag calls arrive and how long each takes, so the calculator in Section 4 ties the two together. At its default pilot values (20,000 users, about 40 red-flag calls a day, 20 minutes each), a 24/7 desk needs three nurses per shift, about 15 full-time staff. At the Bureau of National Statistics' 2026 average for nurses, 264,600 tenge a month,74 that is about 48 million tenge a year in wages. This is an average for the profession; night shifts and the skills a triage desk needs may raise it. Both figures are wages only. Employer costs, a reserve for sickness and leave, phone lines, equipment, maintenance, doctors' time for the answer bank, audits and testing in Kazakh are not calculated, so I cannot yet say what the service costs per year or which item is largest. Who pays: the state, through the Ministry of Health or the health insurance fund. Who profits: the IT company that builds and maintains it (Damumed, one of the leading providers, already runs systems for 2,500 medical organizations),45 hardware suppliers and, if Ana runs inside Telegram or WhatsApp, foreign platforms. Assessment: medium, with a full costing as a task for the pilot; the nurse desk must not be the first line cut.

Labor: whose work changes, whose work is hidden

Nurses move to a queue the AI prioritizes, which shifts responsibility; polyclinics receive more alerts, including false ones. The hidden labor is large: doctors writing and reviewing thousands of answers, Kazakh and Russian annotators labelling messages, testers playing out emergencies, and reviewers reading real chats about miscarriage, stillbirth or violence, work that carries a psychological cost. The hybrid draws on city nurses working remotely, so it does not pull staff out of villages, but over-referral could push extra work onto rural polyclinics. The biggest labor risk is political: a ministry could treat Ana as a reason to cut home visits. Assessment: medium. Ana must add to patronage nursing, not replace it.

Political and governance: a register of worries

Under Kazakhstan's Law on Artificial Intelligence (No. 230-VIII, in force since 18 January 2026), a system whose failure could seriously harm people's lives is high-risk, and its owner classifies it (Art. 17).48,36 Ana is high-risk. The EU AI Act agrees: it lists emergency healthcare patient triage as high-risk in Annex III.50,51 The personal data law requires storage in Kazakhstan,38 which a locally hosted KAZ-LLM satisfies but a Telegram bot may not.

The deeper problem is who controls the record. My choice of IIN at registration may make follow-up faster, which the pilot has to test, but it creates a state-held, identity-linked record of what women ask about their bodies: an unwanted pregnancy, a husband's violence, dark thoughts after birth. Buribayeva et al. show that Kazakhstan's Digital Family Card already profiles households, including health variables, and that "technologically nothing prevents" secondary uses of such profiles. They also warn that one ministry acting as regulator, operator and promoter of AI weakens outside oversight.52 Ana's data could flow into exactly this infrastructure, and Damumed has already faced accusations of exposing personal data.44 Roberts et al. note that broad AI principles are vague and rarely enforced,53 so the table below turns each Week 12 framework into a concrete requirement. Data ethics: consent, babies, and the cost of a false "you're fine"

Ana needs health data from mothers and about babies, who cannot consent; some mothers are themselves minors. Consent must be separate and explicit for three things: using the service, escalating to a nurse, and any use of chats to improve the model. Sensitive topics (abortion, violence, mental health) should never be reported onward except where a life is in danger. Ana's own rule is that chats go only to medical workers, with the mother's consent in the app, never to police or employers. But an app cannot promise more than the law: the Health Code allows medical secrets to be disclosed without consent in some cases, including requests from investigators or a court in connection with a case, and where there are grounds to believe harm was caused by an unlawful act.68 Chats are not sent automatically, but a lawful request could reach them. Closing that door needs a change in the law (my condition 3), and the app must say so at sign-up. What could go wrong is serious. A false reassurance can delay an ambulance; Bean et al. show that a chatbot's medical knowledge does not protect users from wrong decisions.33 A false alarm wears out nurses and mothers' trust. Weaker performance in Kazakh would mean the women with the least access get the worst answers. Households without mobile internet (3.3%46) depend on the SMS fallback, which is less private than an encrypted app. Assessment: high.

Verdict

Yes, but only as a pilot under conditions, and no if the state won't firewall the data

The bill shows a cheap system with expensive failure modes. The environmental and economic costs are manageable. The political and ethical costs are not manageable by default: they depend on choices the state may not want to make. So Kazakhstan should build Ana only if it accepts all of these conditions:

  1. Humans decide every emergency. A remote 24/7 nurse desk, staffed from cities, calls back on every red flag and coordinates with 103; the local patronage nurse or feldsher follows up in the morning. The AI never diagnoses or prescribes.
  2. Test with real mothers first. Run user testing in Kazakh and Russian, as Bean et al. recommend, and publish the missed-red-flag rate before launch.
  3. A legal firewall. Data stays in Kazakhstan, is never used for training without separate consent, and a change in the law ensures it cannot be shared with police, employers, or state profiles such as the Digital Family Card, except to save a life. This is a proposal: today the Health Code lets investigators and courts request medical records in connection with a case, so the app alone cannot promise it, and it must tell mothers so.
  4. An independent auditor outside the Ministry of AI and Digital Development, with the power to suspend the system, so regulator and operator are not the same body.
  5. A human route, always. Mothers can refuse the AI and reach a nurse, as the AI Law allows, and no mood or depression detection happens without explicit consent.
  6. Pilot where the gaps are. Start in two regions with weak follow-up, such as Aktobe and Akmola, plus a comparison region.
  7. Measure outcomes, not users. Track missed red flags, over-referrals, callback times and the time polyclinics spend per visit; expand to other regions, and from babies under one to children under three, only if the pilot meets targets set in advance. The callback promise is one of them: for example, 9 in 10 red flags called back within 5 minutes.
  8. Add, don't replace. Patronage nurses and home visits keep their budgets.

If the state will not accept conditions 3 and 4, my answer is no. A system that turns mothers' private fears into an identity-linked state record, without independent oversight, would cost more in trust than it saves in emergencies, and women who stop asking honest questions are worse off than women who ask Momslife.

AI transparency statement

What the AI did, and what I decided

My contribution

I chose the problem and made the design decisions. I moved through several rejected topics (an AI inspector for contractors' road and sidewalk work, a bus-route chatbot, preventive road-crack detection, a fall-detection bracelet) before choosing maternal health, partly from my own experience of pregnancy in 2024. I tested the Supermom Academy app myself, found it broken, and questioned its 400,000-user claim. I noticed that mothers use Momslife and chatbots rather than state tools. I identified the problems general chatbots have with local context (over-diagnosis, foreign emergency numbers), argued that Ana beats a phone hotline because it works by text and remembers the child's history, supplied what I know as a mother about how often infants are seen, asked for the comparison dialogues and escalation walkthrough, wrote the opening dialogue on the first screen, and proposed the illness log for hospital admission, so a mother does not have to remember every vomit, dose and diaper. I designed the core: self-registration rather than clinic enrollment, escalation to a human on red flags, and IIN at registration so the woman and the nurse know her attached polyclinic, a choice I kept after weighing its privacy cost.

Claude's contribution (Anthropic, Claude)

Claude searched for and summarized sources, ran a structured search of Kazakhstan's maternal health data, checked facts such as the AI law and energy figures, drafted the text of the four sections, and wrote the code for this website and its figures. It also argued against several of my ideas and pointed out weaknesses, such as the privacy cost of IIN-linked data.

What I accepted and what I rejected

I accepted Claude's suggestion to narrow the project to one function and leave out child growth tracking. I accepted the reframing from "rural access" to regional gaps and the time between visits after seeing the MICS data. I rejected the option of registering by phone number only and kept the IIN so her attached polyclinic is known. I rejected Claude's first version of the chatbot comparison as an exaggerated strawman: general chatbots usually get the medicine right, so the final version shows a competent one and locates the difference in what happens after the answer. I rejected dropping the project in favor of easier topics. I supplied the Week 12 readings and legal texts (OECD, UNESCO, the EU AI Act, Kazakhstan's AI Law and AI Concept, Buribayeva et al., Roberts et al.) that the governance assessment is built on. I chose the hybrid escalation model (a remote nurse desk plus the local patronage nurse or feldsher) after weighing three options Claude laid out.
